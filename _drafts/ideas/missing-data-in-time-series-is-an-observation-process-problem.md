A missing value in a time series is often treated as a blank cell. That is sometimes correct. It is also often the wrong abstraction.

In a temporal system there are at least two processes: the process that evolves, and the process by which we observe it. If a sensor fails when vibration is high, if a wearable stops recording when the battery is low, if a patient removes a device when symptoms worsen, or if a database samples more often during alarms, then the pattern of missingness is partly generated by the system.

In those settings the data are not merely incomplete. The observation mechanism is informative.

## Start with a latent state and an observation equation

A simple local-level model is

$$
x_t = x_{t-1} + eta_t,
$$

$$
y_t = x_t + epsilon_t,
$$

with process noise (eta_t) and observation noise (epsilon_t).

Now introduce an observation indicator

$$
r_t in {0,1},
$$

where (r_t=1) means (y_t) is observed.

If

$$
P(r_t=1 mid x_t, y_t, mathcal H_{t-1})
$$

depends on the latent or observed state, then the missingness pattern contains information about the trajectory.

The missingness mechanism is now part of the model.

## Interpolation answers a different question

Linear interpolation estimates a visually plausible path between neighbouring observations. It does not generally reconstruct the conditional distribution of the missing state.

Suppose an AR(1) process follows

$$
x_t = phi x_{t-1} + eta_t.
$$

If a block of values is missing, the conditional expectation under the AR(1) model is not generally a straight line between endpoints. Its uncertainty also expands inside the gap.

Interpolation replaces that uncertainty with a deterministic curve.

For plotting, that may be fine.

For estimating volatility, autocorrelation, threshold-crossing probability, or downstream uncertainty, it can be badly misleading.

## Informative dropout can reverse conclusions

Consider a sensor that becomes unreliable when the process exceeds a high threshold. The observed data will systematically underrepresent extreme states.

An imputation algorithm trained only on observed values can then reconstruct missing points toward the centre of the observed distribution.

The resulting series looks cleaner and less volatile than reality.

This creates a particularly dangerous failure mode: the better the imputer is at reproducing the observed distribution, the more effectively it can erase the unobserved extreme regime.

The problem is not imputation accuracy.

It is identification.

## Regular and irregular sampling are not the same problem

A missing value on a fixed grid differs from an observation that was never scheduled.

In regularly sampled sensor data, we often know that an observation opportunity existed and failed.

In irregular event data, the time between observations may itself be informative.

A hospital measurement taken every four hours during deterioration and every twelve hours during stability is not simply a regularly sampled signal with missing rows.

The sampling policy reflects the patient's state.

Resampling onto a uniform grid can therefore destroy information about clinical attention, operating mode, or event intensity.

## State-space models separate dynamics from measurement

One reason state-space models are useful is that they keep the latent process and observation process distinct.

Filtering estimates

$$
p(x_t mid y_{1:t}),
$$

while smoothing estimates

$$
p(x_t mid y_{1:T}).
$$

When an observation is missing, the update step can be skipped while uncertainty propagates through the transition model.

That is fundamentally different from inventing a pseudo-observation and then pretending it was measured.

This distinction matters when downstream decisions depend on uncertainty.

## Missingness can itself be a feature

Sometimes the missingness pattern is predictive.

A device going silent may signal failure.

A patient stopping diary entries may correlate with worsening symptoms.

A machine entering a maintenance state may suppress sensors that otherwise generate data.

Using missingness as a predictor can therefore be sensible.

But prediction and causal interpretation must be separated.

A model may exploit a missingness indicator successfully while remaining non-transportable if the observation policy changes.

If a hospital changes how often nurses record vital signs, a model using measurement frequency as a severity proxy may fail without any physiological shift.

## The downstream estimand should decide the treatment

There is no universally correct way to handle a temporal gap.

For forecasting, preserving dynamics may matter most.

For estimating a mean over a long interval, a simple method may be sufficient.

For change-point detection, smoothing can erase the very discontinuity of interest.

For causal inference, imputing post-treatment variables can create bias.

For anomaly detection, replacing a missing burst with normal-looking values can suppress the alarm.

The imputation method should therefore be chosen after the target quantity is defined.

That is the same principle that applies elsewhere in statistics: the estimand comes before the algorithm.

## A practical hierarchy

Before filling a gap, ask:

1. Was an observation expected at this time?
2. What mechanism could explain its absence?
3. Could that mechanism depend on the latent state?
4. Does the downstream analysis use dynamics, variability, thresholds, or uncertainty?
5. Would a deterministic fill be interpreted later as if it had been observed?

If the answers suggest informative missingness, the observation process deserves explicit modelling.

## Conclusion

Missing data in time series are not always damaged values waiting to be reconstructed.

Sometimes they are evidence about the system that generated the data.

Interpolation, smoothing, state-space filtering and missingness-aware likelihoods answer different questions. The correct choice depends on the observation mechanism and the downstream estimand.

A gap is not merely an empty coordinate.

It can be part of the process.
