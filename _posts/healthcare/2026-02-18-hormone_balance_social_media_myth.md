The phrase *hormone imbalance* sounds precise while often being almost empty.

It appears in posts about fatigue, acne, difficulty losing weight, poor sleep, low libido, premenstrual symptoms, anxiety, irregular cycles and menopause. The diagnosis is frequently inferred from a checklist, and the proposed solution is equally broad: “balance your hormones”.

That language hides the central fact of endocrinology.

There is no single hormonal balance.

The endocrine system consists of multiple feedback loops, rhythms and tissue-specific signals. Thyroid hormones, insulin, cortisol, prolactin, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone, luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone do not move together around one ideal centre.

A useful clinical question is never simply “are your hormones balanced?”

It is: **which hormone, measured when, in what physiological state, using which assay, and interpreted against which clinical question?**

Without that specificity, “hormone imbalance” becomes a label that can explain almost anything and therefore diagnoses very little.

## Endocrine physiology is dynamic by design

Hormones are signals.

They rise and fall in response to feedback, time of day, sleep, food intake, physical activity, reproductive state, medication and disease.

The menstrual cycle is an obvious example. Estradiol, progesterone, LH and FSH vary substantially across the cycle. Those changes are not evidence that the system is unstable. They are the system working normally.

Cortisol has a pronounced daily rhythm. Insulin changes minute by minute with nutrient intake. Thyroid hormones are regulated by hypothalamic and pituitary feedback. Reproductive hormones change across puberty, pregnancy, lactation and menopause.

This makes the word *balance* biologically misleading because it suggests a static target.

Many healthy endocrine systems are healthy precisely because they change.

The important question is whether the pattern of regulation is appropriate for the person's physiological state.

## Symptoms are not hormone tests

Online hormone content often begins with symptom lists.

Tiredness, poor concentration, irritability, acne, low mood, cravings, weight gain and poor sleep are presented as evidence of hormonal dysfunction.

These symptoms are real, but they are not specific.

Fatigue can accompany anaemia, infection, sleep deprivation, depression, medication effects, thyroid disease and dozens of other conditions. Acne can reflect androgen sensitivity, skin biology, medication or ordinary variation. Weight change has multiple possible causes.

A symptom becomes diagnostically useful when it changes the probability of a specific disorder in context.

The more common and non-specific the symptom, the weaker it is as a standalone diagnostic signal.

This is why endocrine diagnosis uses a combination of history, examination, appropriately timed laboratory testing and sometimes imaging rather than broad symptom matching.

A vague symptom list can generate suspicion.

It cannot identify an endocrine axis.

## Timing changes what a hormone result means

Hormone tests are unusually vulnerable to decontextualisation.

A value that is normal at one time may be unexpected at another.

Sex hormones can vary across the menstrual cycle. Cortisol varies across the day. Some hormones are pulsatile. Menopause introduces further variability as ovarian function becomes less predictable.

ACOG notes that hormone testing before starting menopausal hormone therapy is generally not recommended because levels fluctuate substantially during the menopausal transition and often do not add useful information to the clinical decision.

That does not mean hormone testing is useless.

It means testing must answer a defined question.

A laboratory number is only as informative as the physiological model used to interpret it.

## Saliva testing creates an illusion of personalisation

One of the strongest commercial expressions of “hormone balancing” is salivary testing used to customise hormone therapy.

The idea is attractive: measure the individual's hormone profile, identify what is “low” or “high”, then prescribe a personalised mixture.

Professional guidance is much more cautious.

ACOG states that data supporting adjunct hormone testing for prescribing compounded menopausal hormone therapy are limited and does not recommend such testing for that purpose. Its 2023 consensus notes that salivary measurements of estrogen and progesterone do not provide sufficiently accurate or precise information to guide custom dosing in the way commonly advertised.

The Endocrine Society reaches a similar conclusion. It states that serial salivary or blood testing has no demonstrated role in customising compounded menopausal hormone therapy, except where specific endocrine conditions require validated monitoring.

The broader lesson is important.

Personalisation is not achieved merely by adding a test.

A personalised test is useful only if the assay is valid, the target range is meaningful, the measurement changes management and changing management improves outcomes.

Without that chain, personalisation becomes precision theatre.

## “Bioidentical” is a chemical description, not a safety guarantee

The term *bioidentical hormone* is another place where scientific language and marketing overlap.

A bioidentical hormone has the same chemical structure as a hormone produced by the body. That fact does not imply that all products marketed with the term are equally safe, effective or well controlled.

FDA-approved estradiol and micronised progesterone can be bioidentical.

Custom-compounded formulations can also contain bioidentical hormones.

The difference is not whether the molecule sounds natural. It is manufacturing, dose consistency, quality control, evidence and regulatory oversight.

ACOG and the Endocrine Society both caution against routine use of custom-compounded menopausal hormone products when approved alternatives exist. They note that compounded preparations can vary in potency and purity and lack the same standardised evidence base.

The Menopause Society likewise emphasises that custom-compounded products are not inherently safer or more effective than approved bioidentical therapies.

This is a useful example of why the word *natural* contributes very little to pharmacology.

Chemical identity matters.

So do dose, route, quality control and evidence.

## Cycle syncing is plausible in principle and overstated in practice

Another popular version of hormone optimisation is *cycle syncing*: adjusting exercise, diet or productivity according to menstrual-cycle phase.

The idea is biologically plausible because ovarian hormones fluctuate across the cycle and can affect thermoregulation, substrate use, symptoms and other physiological variables.

The practical evidence is much less dramatic.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 78 studies examining exercise performance across the menstrual cycle found that performance may be trivially lower in the early follicular phase, but the average effect was small, between-study variability was large and the quality of evidence was low.

The authors concluded that general exercise prescriptions based solely on menstrual phase could not be justified and recommended an individualised approach.

That conclusion is very different from the rigid schedules circulating online.

Some people experience substantial cycle-related symptoms and may reasonably adjust training around them.

Others do not.

Averages do not erase individual experience, but individual experience also does not justify universal rules.

The sensible interpretation is that cycle tracking may be useful for self-observation, while strong phase-specific performance prescriptions remain poorly supported.

## Symptoms across the cycle can matter more than the phase label

Recent research on menstrual symptoms and recreational physical activity reinforces this point.

A 2024 systematic review involving thousands of participants found that symptoms, lived experience and social factors influenced participation in physical activity.

That is clinically and practically important.

It also shows why “phase X requires workout Y” is too mechanical.

Two people can be in the same nominal cycle phase and experience very different symptoms, sleep, pain and motivation.

A flexible approach based on actual response may therefore be more useful than treating the cycle as a fixed four-stage training algorithm.

The body is not a calendar template.

## Seed cycling is a good example of premature certainty

Seed cycling involves eating different seeds during different phases of the menstrual cycle, usually flax and pumpkin seeds during one phase and sesame and sunflower seeds during another.

The proposed mechanisms involve lignans, fatty acids, micronutrients and possible effects on estrogen or progesterone metabolism.

The evidence is emerging but weak relative to the confidence of many online claims.

A 2025 systematic review identified ten studies involving 635 participants with PMS or PCOS. The authors reported potentially favourable effects on menstrual regularity, symptoms and some metabolic or hormonal markers, but also emphasised small samples, heterogeneous methods and the need for larger standardised randomized trials.

The result is not “seed cycling is nonsense”.

It is also not “seed cycling balances hormones”.

A separate 2023 meta-analysis of randomized flaxseed trials found no significant overall effect on several sex-hormone measures including FSH, total testosterone, free androgen index and DHEAS.

Those findings are not necessarily contradictory because the interventions, populations and outcomes differ.

They do show why confident mechanistic claims are premature.

The seeds themselves are nutritious foods.

The phase-specific hormonal theory remains much less established.

## Hormonal disorders are real

Criticising vague “hormone balance” language should not become a denial of endocrine disease.

Hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Cushing syndrome, adrenal insufficiency, hyperprolactinaemia, PCOS and many other disorders are real, measurable and clinically important.

They are diagnosed by identifying a specific abnormality within a defined physiological system.

That specificity is exactly what the wellness phrase often lacks.

A person with thyroid disease does not have generic “hormone imbalance”. They have a disorder involving a particular axis with a particular pattern of laboratory and clinical findings.

Precision improves care because it narrows the mechanism.

Vagueness does the opposite.

## Supplements often exploit the gap between symptom and diagnosis

The commercial appeal of hormone-balancing supplements follows from the breadth of the diagnosis.

If the symptom list includes fatigue, poor sleep, mood change, weight gain and low libido, a large percentage of adults can recognise themselves.

The supplement can then be marketed as supporting estrogen metabolism, progesterone, testosterone, thyroid function or adrenal resilience without demonstrating that the buyer has a disorder involving any of those systems.

Mechanistic ingredients may indeed affect enzymes or signalling pathways.

That is not equivalent to treating a diagnosed endocrine condition.

The relevant clinical sequence should be:

identify the problem, establish whether an endocrine abnormality exists, determine whether it is causal, then test whether the intervention improves an outcome.

Commercial hormone balancing often reverses the sequence: the product exists first, and the diagnosis is broad enough to create the market.

## One laboratory panel cannot summarise endocrine health

Another feature of the hormone-balance market is the large multipanel test.

A dozen hormones are measured at once, and values near the edge of a reference range are interpreted as evidence of dysfunction.

Reference intervals do not work that way.

A reference range usually describes the distribution of measurements in a selected population under particular laboratory conditions. It is not a universal optimal zone, and values near the boundary are not automatically pathological.

When many biomarkers are measured simultaneously, some results will fall outside reference limits by chance alone.

That statistical fact becomes more important as panel size increases.

Clinical interpretation therefore depends on pre-test probability, symptoms, assay quality, biological variation and the consequences of acting on the result.

Testing more variables does not automatically generate more useful information.

Sometimes it only generates more opportunities to find something to “correct”.

## The phrase “balance hormones naturally” combines several weak ideas

The rhetoric usually rests on three assumptions.

First, that hormones should be pushed toward a central ideal rather than allowed to vary normally.

Second, that natural interventions are inherently gentler or safer than pharmacological ones.

Third, that changing a hormone concentration is automatically beneficial.

None is generally valid.

Hormone systems are regulated dynamically.

Natural compounds can have strong pharmacological effects.

And a biomarker change is not necessarily a clinical improvement.

The word *balance* hides all three problems because it sounds self-evidently desirable.

Few people want an “unbalanced” body.

That is what makes the phrase so effective as marketing.

## A better way to think about endocrine claims

A useful endocrine claim should identify the axis, the expected physiology and the clinical endpoint.

If someone says a supplement “balances estrogen”, the next questions are immediate.

Which estrogen measure?

In which population?

At what point in the cycle or reproductive state?

Compared with what?

Does the intervention improve symptoms or disease outcomes?

What happens when the baseline level is already normal?

Those questions are not pedantic.

They convert a marketing phrase into a scientific proposition.

And sometimes the proposition turns out to be useful.

Sometimes it disappears once it has to be defined.

## Conclusion

Hormonal disorders are real, but “hormone imbalance” is often too vague to be a diagnosis.

Hormones are not one system moving around a single point of balance. They belong to multiple feedback loops, fluctuate across time and reproductive state, and can only be interpreted in context.

Salivary testing does not become useful merely because it is personalised. Compounded “bioidentical” hormones are not made safer by the word natural. Cycle syncing has plausible biology but weak evidence for rigid universal prescriptions. Seed cycling is an interesting emerging idea, not an established method for restoring hormonal balance.

The better question is always more specific:

**which hormone, which axis, measured when, and linked to which clinical outcome?**

Endocrinology becomes clearer as soon as the word *balance* is replaced by an actual physiological question.
