Few health phrases are as visually effective as *leaky gut*. The metaphor suggests a damaged barrier through which unwanted material enters the bloodstream and produces systemic symptoms. It is simple, memorable and flexible enough to explain almost anything: fatigue, bloating, acne, anxiety, autoimmune disease, brain fog, joint pain and weight gain.

The difficulty is that the underlying physiology is real.

The intestine is a selectively permeable barrier. Its epithelial cells, mucus, tight junctions, immune system, antimicrobial peptides and microbiota interact to allow nutrient absorption while limiting uncontrolled passage of luminal material.

Permeability can increase in defined diseases and stress states.

That does not establish a single syndrome capable of explaining every symptom attributed to it online.

The distinction between those two claims is the entire subject.

## The intestinal barrier is dynamic

A healthy intestine is not impermeable.

If the barrier blocked all movement, nutrient and water absorption would fail. The physiological objective is selective transport combined with effective host defence.

Barrier function therefore changes with tissue location, immune state, microbial activity, diet, medication and physiological stress.

This is why the language of an intestinal wall being simply “open” or “closed” is misleading. Permeability is a property of a dynamic system, not a binary defect.

Camilleri's review of the human intestinal barrier emphasises this complexity and the difficulty of measuring it with a single test.

That matters because online discussions often treat permeability as though it were one latent quantity that can be inferred from symptoms alone.

## Increased permeability occurs in real disease

Coeliac disease provides a clear example.

In genetically susceptible people, gluten exposure triggers an immune-mediated enteropathy. Increased intestinal permeability accompanies the disease process and can improve after treatment with a gluten-free diet.

Inflammatory bowel disease also involves barrier dysfunction, epithelial injury and immune activation.

Those are established clinical conditions with defined diagnostic frameworks.

They should not be confused with the broader wellness category “leaky gut syndrome”.

The scientific problem is not whether permeability can be abnormal. It can.

The problem is whether a heterogeneous collection of non-specific symptoms can be assigned to barrier dysfunction without validated diagnostic evidence.

That is a much stronger claim.

## Non-specific symptoms are poor diagnostic markers

Fatigue, bloating, headache, anxiety and difficulty concentrating occur in many conditions.

A symptom becomes diagnostically useful when it meaningfully changes the probability of a specific disease in context.

The online leaky-gut model often reverses this logic. Because a symptom is common among people discussing gut health, it is treated as evidence of intestinal permeability.

That inference is weak.

Bloating can result from altered motility, fermentation, constipation, visceral hypersensitivity, meal volume or several gastrointestinal disorders. Fatigue can reflect sleep, anaemia, infection, mood disorder, medication, endocrine disease or many other causes.

The fact that barrier dysfunction can coexist with some of these states does not make the symptom a permeability test.

A broad syndrome constructed from common symptoms will appear prevalent even when the underlying mechanism has not been demonstrated.

## The zonulin problem is a measurement problem

Commercial zonulin testing is one of the clearest examples of how numerical precision can exceed biological certainty.

Zonulin attracted interest because it was proposed as a regulator of tight junctions and therefore appeared to offer a convenient blood marker of intestinal permeability.

Several studies subsequently raised a fundamental analytical problem: widely used commercial zonulin ELISA kits may not be measuring pre-haptoglobin-2, the protein originally described as zonulin.

Work using immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry suggested that some assays detect other proteins, including properdin or related molecules.

That matters because a test result cannot be interpreted confidently if the analyte itself is uncertain.

Further studies have reported poor correlation between commercial serum “zonulin” values and physiological permeability measures such as the lactulose-mannitol test.

A number can therefore be precise while the inference attached to it remains weak.

This is a general lesson in laboratory medicine: validity requires more than a decimal point.

## Permeability itself is difficult to measure

There is no perfect single test for intestinal permeability.

Sugar-probe tests such as lactulose-mannitol ratios have been used for decades, but they are affected by intestinal transit, renal function, urine collection, dose and timing.

Other approaches use biopsies, tissue resistance, circulating markers, microbial products or combinations of biomarkers.

A recent review of intestinal-permeability biomarkers lists multiple candidates rather than one accepted universal test. That diversity reflects the complexity of the biology.

It also explains why commercial claims that a single blood test can diagnose “leaky gut” should be treated cautiously.

The measurement problem comes before the treatment problem.

## Increased permeability can be temporary and adaptive

Exercise offers another useful counterexample to the idea that increased permeability always means disease.

Strenuous endurance exercise, particularly under heat stress, can transiently increase markers of intestinal injury and permeability. Reduced splanchnic blood flow and thermal stress contribute.

That does not mean exercise causes a chronic “leaky gut syndrome”.

It means a physiological stressor can temporarily alter barrier function.

This is the same conceptual mistake seen elsewhere in wellness culture: a transient biological perturbation is treated as if it were equivalent to persistent pathology.

Context and recovery matter.

## Gluten does not provide a universal explanation

The biology of coeliac disease is often generalised to everyone.

For people with coeliac disease, gluten is a defined pathological trigger and a gluten-free diet is treatment.

That fact does not establish that gluten makes every healthy person's intestine pathologically permeable.

Wheat allergy and non-coeliac wheat or gluten sensitivity are separate clinical questions and should not be collapsed into coeliac disease.

The online chain “gluten increases zonulin, therefore gluten causes leaky gut and systemic disease in everyone” transports evidence across populations without justification.

A mechanism demonstrated in a susceptible group cannot automatically be treated as a universal response.

## Diet can influence barrier biology without validating one “gut healing” diet

Dietary composition affects the microbiota, fermentation, mucus, epithelial metabolism and immune signalling.

That makes diet an entirely legitimate variable in barrier research.

But the evidence for a universal “gut healing” diet is much weaker than the rhetoric suggests.

A 2024 systematic review examining dietary interventions and permeability in healthy people found only a small, heterogeneous evidence base. Some interventions, including inulin and probiotics, showed possible effects, but the literature did not support a single general protocol.

This is another example of a common inferential jump: because diet influences barrier biology, one marketed diet is presented as the solution.

The first statement is broad physiology.

The second requires direct comparative evidence.

## Probiotics and glutamine are plausible, but plausibility is not universality

Probiotics are frequently promoted as a way to “seal” the gut.

Meta-analyses of randomized trials have reported improvements in some barrier-related markers with probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics. The results are heterogeneous because strains, populations and endpoints differ.

That heterogeneity matters.

A probiotic is not one intervention. Different strains can have different effects, and a change in a permeability biomarker is not automatically equivalent to improvement in the disease or symptom that matters to the patient.

Glutamine has similar appeal because enterocytes use it as a metabolic substrate.

A 2024 meta-analysis of randomized trials found no significant overall effect of glutamine supplementation on intestinal permeability, although some subgroup analyses suggested possible effects at higher doses or shorter durations.

That pattern is scientifically interesting.

It is not a basis for recommending glutamine universally as a gut-repair treatment.

## The microbiome does not resolve causality

The microbiome has made gut explanations more compelling because it provides another plausible biological layer.

Differences in microbial composition are associated with many diseases.

But association does not identify direction.

Disease can alter the microbiome. Medication can alter both disease and microbiome. Diet can influence both. A microbial difference can be a cause, consequence, marker or mixture of all three.

The presence of microbiome data therefore does not automatically validate a causal narrative about permeability.

It adds another variable that must be interpreted carefully.

## Repairing a biomarker is not the same as curing a disease

Even when permeability is genuinely abnormal, one more inferential step remains.

If disease and permeability are associated, several causal structures are possible. Barrier dysfunction may contribute to the disease. The disease may damage the barrier. A third factor may cause both. Feedback may operate in both directions.

This is why normalising a permeability marker does not automatically establish that the underlying disease has been treated.

The clinically relevant question is whether the intervention improves symptoms, disease activity, complications or quality of life, not merely whether one laboratory marker moves.

That distinction is central to evidence-based medicine and frequently absent from commercial gut-health communication.

## The real science is more interesting than the syndrome

Intestinal-barrier research is an active field involving epithelial junctions, mucosal immunology, microbial metabolites, tissue repair, drug effects, diet and host-microbiome signalling.

That work is important precisely because the system is complex.

Reducing it to “your gut is leaking” discards most of what makes the biology scientifically interesting.

It also creates an almost unfalsifiable commercial model. Symptoms are non-specific, biomarkers are uncertain, treatment endpoints are vague and failure can be explained by saying the gut has not healed enough.

A diagnosis that can absorb every result is difficult to test.

## Conclusion

Intestinal permeability is real.

Barrier dysfunction occurs in coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and other defined states. Intense exercise and some medications can transiently alter permeability. Diet and microbial interventions may influence barrier-related measures.

Those facts do not establish a universal “leaky gut syndrome” that explains fatigue, brain fog, acne, weight gain and autoimmunity across the general population.

The correct hierarchy is slower and less dramatic: first establish the clinical condition, then establish the measurement, then establish the causal role, and only then ask whether changing the barrier improves the outcome.

The existence of a real mechanism does not create a universal diagnosis.
