KL divergence and Wasserstein distance both compare probability distributions, but they encode different geometry.

KL divergence depends on density ratios.

Wasserstein distance depends on how far probability mass must move through an underlying metric space.

That distinction determines when each measure is useful.

## KL divergence

For distributions $P$ and $Q$ with densities $p$ and $q$,

$$
D_{KL}(P\|Q)
=
\int
p(x)
\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
\,dx.
$$

For discrete distributions,

$$
D_{KL}(P\|Q)
=
\sum_x
p(x)
\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
$$

KL divergence is always nonnegative and is zero only when the distributions agree almost everywhere.

It is not symmetric:

$$
D_{KL}(P\|Q)
\ne
D_{KL}(Q\|P).
$$

Therefore it is not a metric.

## Support matters

If

$$
p(x)>0
$$

where

$$
q(x)=0,
$$

then

$$
D_{KL}(P\|Q)=\infty.
$$

This is not a numerical inconvenience; it is a structural property.

Using $Q$ to encode outcomes that $P$ can produce but $Q$ assigns zero probability incurs infinite log-loss.

## Information-theoretic interpretation

If data are generated from $P$ but encoded using a code optimized for $Q$, the expected excess log-loss is governed by KL divergence.

This is the sense in which KL measures information inefficiency.

It should not be casually described as a geometric distance.

## Direction matters in variational inference

Variational inference often minimizes

$$
D_{KL}(Q\|P).
$$

That direction tends to penalize placing mass where $P$ has little support.

The reverse direction has different behavior.

The two objectives can produce very different approximations to multimodal targets.

## Wasserstein distance

Let $(\mathcal X,d)$ be a metric space.

The $p$-Wasserstein distance is

$$
W_p(P,Q)
=
\left[
\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(P,Q)}
\int
d(x,y)^p
\,d\gamma(x,y)
\right]^{1/p},
$$

where $\Gamma(P,Q)$ is the set of couplings with marginals $P$ and $Q$.

It asks for the cheapest way to transport mass from one distribution to the other.

## Geometry matters

Suppose

$$
P=\delta_0,
\qquad
Q=\delta_\varepsilon.
$$

The two point masses have disjoint support.

KL divergence is infinite in either direction.

But

$$
W_1(P,Q)=|\varepsilon|.
$$

As $\varepsilon\to0$, Wasserstein distance goes to zero smoothly.

This is one reason Wasserstein methods can behave better when distributions move continuously through space while their supports remain disjoint.

## One-dimensional formula

For one-dimensional distributions with quantile functions $F^{-1}$ and $G^{-1}$,

$$
W_p(P,Q)^p
=
\int_0^1
|F^{-1}(u)-G^{-1}(u)|^p
\,du.
$$

This gives a direct interpretation as average quantile displacement.

## Scale dependence

Wasserstein distance inherits the units of the underlying space.

If $x$ is measured in meters, $W_1$ is measured in meters.

KL divergence is dimensionless.

This makes Wasserstein interpretation intuitive in some applications, but it also means feature scaling strongly affects multivariate results.

## Estimation in high dimension

Empirical Wasserstein distances can converge slowly in high-dimensional spaces.

This is an important limitation.

The geometric appeal of Wasserstein distance does not eliminate the curse of dimensionality.

Regularized optimal transport and sliced Wasserstein methods are common approximations.

## GAN terminology

Wasserstein GANs are motivated by Wasserstein-1 geometry, but implementations typically optimize a dual objective under Lipschitz constraints rather than computing an exact primal transport plan.

Saying that WGANs directly calculate Earth Mover distance at every step is therefore too simplistic.

## A simple discrete KL calculation

~~~python
from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray


def kl_divergence(
    p: NDArray[np.float64],
    q: NDArray[np.float64],
) -> float:
    """Compute D_KL(P || Q) for discrete probability vectors."""
    if p.shape != q.shape or p.ndim != 1:
        raise ValueError("p and q must be one-dimensional with equal shape")
    if np.any(p < 0) or np.any(q < 0):
        raise ValueError("probabilities must be non-negative")
    if not np.isclose(p.sum(), 1.0) or not np.isclose(q.sum(), 1.0):
        raise ValueError("p and q must each sum to one")

    positive = p > 0
    if np.any(q[positive] == 0):
        return float("inf")

    return float(np.sum(p[positive] * np.log(p[positive] / q[positive])))
~~~

## Choosing between them

Use KL when density ratios, log-loss, likelihood, coding, or variational objectives are central.

Use Wasserstein when the geometry of how probability mass moves is scientifically meaningful.

Neither is universally better.

They answer different mathematical questions.

## Conclusion

The main distinction is:

$$
\text{KL: density-ratio discrepancy}
$$

versus

$$
\text{Wasserstein: transport cost}.
$$

That difference explains their behavior under support mismatch, scaling, high-dimensional estimation, and model optimization.
