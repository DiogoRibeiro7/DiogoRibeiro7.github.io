A p-value is a probability calculated under a statistical model.

It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

It is not the probability that the observed result occurred by chance.

And it is not a measure of effect size.

## Definition

Let $T(X)$ be a test statistic and suppose larger values are more incompatible with the null hypothesis $H_0$.

For observed value $t_{obs}$, a one-sided p-value is

$$
p
=
P_{H_0}
\left(
T(X)\ge t_{obs}
\right).
$$

A two-sided p-value requires a definition of what counts as “at least as extreme” in both directions.

That definition depends on the test.

## The probability is conditional on the null

The p-value is

$$
P(\text{data as or more extreme}\mid H_0).
$$

It is not

$$
P(H_0\mid\text{data}).
$$

Reversing those conditional probabilities is one of the most common statistical errors.

## Small p-values

A small p-value means the observed test statistic would be unusual under the null model and sampling assumptions.

That can be evidence against the null.

But the strength of evidence depends on design quality, model assumptions, multiplicity, prior plausibility, and whether the test was selected after looking at the data.

## Large p-values

A large p-value does not prove that the null is true.

It may mean:

- data are compatible with the null
- the study has low power
- the estimate is imprecise
- the effect is small
- the test is poorly matched to the data

Absence of statistical significance is not evidence of equivalence.

Equivalence requires an equivalence design or interval-based criterion.

## Effect size and uncertainty

Suppose a mean difference estimate is

$$
\widehat\Delta=0.2
$$

with a very large sample.

The p-value can be tiny even when the effect is practically irrelevant.

Conversely, a large but imprecise effect can have a large p-value in a small sample.

Report the estimate and interval, not only the test decision.

## The significance threshold is a decision rule

The conventional threshold

$$
\alpha=0.05
$$

is not a law of nature.

It controls Type I error under repeated use of a prespecified procedure.

Choosing $\alpha$ should depend on the scientific and decision context.

## Multiple testing

If many hypotheses are tested, some small p-values occur by chance.

Family-wise error control and false discovery rate procedures address different goals.

Running 100 unadjusted tests and highlighting the smallest p-value is not equivalent to one prespecified test.

## Optional stopping and researcher degrees of freedom

Repeatedly checking significance and stopping when

$$
p<0.05
$$

changes the null distribution of the procedure.

Likewise, trying many outcomes, transformations, subgroups, and model specifications can invalidate naive p-values.

The whole analysis path matters.

## Exact, asymptotic, and permutation p-values

Not all p-values are constructed the same way.

They may come from:

- exact finite-sample distributions
- asymptotic approximations
- permutation distributions
- bootstrap approximations

Each requires different assumptions.

A p-value is only as valid as the reference distribution used to compute it.

## Confidence intervals and p-values

For many standard two-sided tests, rejecting

$$
H_0:\theta=\theta_0
$$

at level $\alpha$ is equivalent to a $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval excluding $\theta_0$.

The interval is usually more informative because it shows effect size and precision.

## Bayesian probabilities are different objects

A Bayesian posterior probability such as

$$
P(H_0\mid y)
$$

requires a prior and a Bayesian model.

It is not obtained by transforming a frequentist p-value.

Likewise, Bayes factors and p-values answer different questions.

## A simple simulation intuition

Under a continuous null model and a correctly specified test, p-values are approximately Uniform(0,1):

$$
P(p\le\alpha\mid H_0)=\alpha.
$$

That is the repeated-sampling calibration property behind significance tests.

## Conclusion

The safest interpretation is:

> A p-value measures how surprising a test statistic is under a specified null model.

It does not tell us whether the null is probably true, whether the effect matters, or whether the study design was good.

Those questions require additional evidence.
